# Pushback & Resolutions: *Deterrence by Presence* & *The Grammar of Genocide* Drafting Session

A record of every point in this thread where John pushed back on a Claude proposal, and how each was resolved.

This session built two puzzles in one continuous thread — *Deterrence by Presence* (PR #147, Srebrenica) and *The Grammar of Genocide* (PR #148, a five-genocide comparative capstone that grew directly out of pushback on the first). It runs in four movements: tone and honesty (dramatic framing, euphemism, missing historical depth); scope and substance (whether a single-event puzzle was even the right unit, and what claim the resulting comparative puzzle was actually entitled to make); five rounds correcting one recurring failure in lens design, resolved only once the actual rule got checked directly instead of guessed at; and a long final stretch of close editorial review — schema mechanics, prose precision, sourcing, and rendering — that kept finding problems the earlier rounds hadn't reached yet.

---

## Part 1: Deterrence by Presence

### Round 1: Missing the years before the crisis

> "There's a lot of high drama here, but not a lot of reflection on what led up to the situation over many years prior?"

**Resolution:** Added a fourth cluster, *The Corridor*, covering the 1992–93 strategic goal and ethnic-cleansing campaign that preceded the five-day collapse, plus two new bridges connecting it to the existing clusters. Rewrote the learning introduction to open in 1992 rather than 1993. Set `large: true` to fit the added content.

### Round 2: Play-by-play instead of anatomy

> "My concern is that you're not holding on to the ethos of the entire series, but rather treating certain puzzles almost as akin to a play-by-play of some kind of extended sporting event?"

**Resolution:** Agreed the "request chain" and "too-late-to-matter" lenses, and several bridge facts, were structured as countdown suspense rather than structural analysis. Reframed the lenses (*built-in-limits*, *decisions-not-accidents*), rewrote the hostage-taking and close-air-support facts to describe design rather than drama, cut the single purely anecdotal term (a misrouted request form), and banked a standing memory principle: board facts state mechanism and what it reveals; narrative color belongs in citations and links.

### Round 3: The books already do the drama well

> "Because there are plenty of other places for the player to read dramatic accounts, and we can even provide helpful links to those? But, yeah, for our series, the focus is elsewhere?"

**Resolution:** Cut the misrouted-request-form term entirely rather than continuing to soften it. Renamed "Bernard Janvier's hesitation" to "the UN veto," moving the individual's name into supporting text rather than making personal psychology the headline. Added a "force protection" term naming the mandate's actual design priority. Added direct links to Rohde, Suljagić, and Honig & Both at both the puzzle and learning-introduction level, with the introduction now explicitly pointing readers to the books for the hour-by-hour account instead of trying to compress it.

### Round 4: An unexamined euphemism used as a bridge

> "The use of the term 'close air support' is not one I was familiar with. It's a term that sanitizes the process of bombing the shit of hostile troops that are close to one's own 'friendly' troops apparently? And this is what we use as a bridge?"

**Resolution:** Agreed directly — the term had been used unchallenged despite an existing standing rule that euphemistic language must be named and critiqued in the surrounding prose, not accepted at face value. Rewrote the bridge's fact and info text to open by naming what the term obscures, and reclassified its `termRole` from connector to reference, since the euphemism itself had become an object of the lesson rather than incidental vocabulary.

## Part 2: Scoping the comparative puzzle

### Round 5: Questioning the whole project's shape

> "This puzzle feels dense. Are we going to create a puzzle for every genocide in history and make it equally dense? What's our end game here?"

**Resolution:** Proposed a standing scope rule — a new puzzle earns inclusion by demonstrating a mechanism not already represented, not by topical coverage of an important event — and a density default (standard size as the expectation, `large` as a signal something needs restructuring rather than a ceiling to grow into). No puzzle edit yet; this set the terms for everything that followed.

### Round 6: The real question underneath the logistics

> "That's interesting and all, but it doesn't ask the question what dark impulse of the soul leads one group to want to kill off another?"

**Resolution:** Gave a direct, substantive answer rather than routing back to puzzle mechanics — grievance fused to a material objective, dehumanization as the hinge, disproportion cited as origin, execution through ordinary bureaucracy. No puzzle edit; this became the seed of the comparative puzzle's actual structure two rounds later.

### Round 7: A check against collecting for its own sake

> "I just want to make sure you're not like one of these old guys who buys the entire Time/Life series on the history of war on VHS/DVD and spends hours and hours watching them all on repeat, the way others might do with the History of [sporting event x]?"

**Resolution:** Drew the real distinction — argument-shaped versus shelf-shaped — and applied it honestly to the session's own recent behavior (a puzzle that had grown a fourth cluster and several lenses without anyone asking whether more was actually better). No resolution required beyond the self-check itself, which set up the next round's redirection.

### Round 8: A different kind of artifact entirely

> "Because if you had asked me how I wanted to proceed, I would have asked if there were common elements, where, instead of isolating each genocide individually, we could have stitched them together into a more comprehensive whole?"

**Resolution:** Proposed a mechanism-based framework (grievance fused to objective, redescription, disproportion cited as origin, ordinary bureaucracy) drawing evidence across multiple genocides per cluster, modeled on the existing *Manufacturing Consent* capstone rather than another single-event puzzle. Asked which cases and what scope before building anything.

### Round 9: Building it

> "Let's build the comparative puzzle separately and then decide what to do with the existing puzzles?"

**Resolution:** Built *The Grammar of Genocide* from scratch — four clusters, three bridges, a learning introduction, three lenses — drawing evidence from Armenia, the Holocaust, Rwanda, Cambodia, and Srebrenica. Submitted as PR #148, deliberately without a catalogue assignment pending the deferred decision about the existing puzzles.

## Part 3: Dehumanization and the puzzle's structure

### Round 10: A missing mechanism

> "The Grammar of Genocide puzzle is compelling. It ties things together in a much more effective way than the other more event-based puzzles. If I were to challenge the narrative at all, it would be in what the framing leaves out. [...] Dehumanization isn't mentioned, but it's an essential core of what occurs in every instance of genocidal behavior?"

**Resolution:** Added a fifth cluster, *Dehumanization*, with terms drawn from four separate cases (Untermenschen, inyenzi, harmful microbes, genetically deformed). Converted the "moral disengagement" bridge to a ternary spanning the objective, redescription, and dehumanization clusters, since Bandura's own framework treats euphemism and dehumanization as distinct mechanisms. Rewrote the learning introduction's central claim and resubmitted at 23 nodes.

### Round 11: A challenge to name a real scholarly objection

> "Point me to a single genocide scholar who would declare the centrality of the term 'dehumanization' as controversial? I'd dare to conjecture that there are none?"

**Resolution:** Separated two different claims — dehumanization's *centrality* (essentially undisputed) from dehumanization as a strict *precondition* other mechanisms depend on (a stronger claim). Researched and cited Christopher Browning's *Ordinary Men*, whose empirical account of one Holocaust killing unit treats conformity and obedience as doing at least as much causal work, and is criticized by others for underweighting dehumanization — real, live disagreement about relative weighting, not centrality. Rewrote the paragraph and added the citation.

### Round 12: A correction to the correction

> "Behavior under duress is categorically different, though, is it not?"

**Resolution:** Recognized a level-of-analysis error: Browning's subjects were already standing inside a system that had spent a decade constructing the legal and propaganda architecture of dehumanization before any of them were given an order. Individual compliance psychology under duress is a different question from what builds the architecture duress operates inside of — and the puzzle's five clusters are entirely about that architecture. Rewrote the paragraph to state this distinction directly and restored full confidence in dehumanization's centrality at the level the puzzle actually maps.

### Round 13: The board didn't say what the text said

> "I don't really like that 'dehumanization' is a parallel cluster to the others, as if all clusters carried equal weight?"

**Resolution:** The sharpest structural catch of the session — the prose argued dehumanization was foundational, not a peer, while the board treated all five clusters identically. Added two new bridges (`classification`, connecting dehumanization to bureaucracy; `collective guilt`, connecting it to disproportion) making Dehumanization the only cluster wired to all four others. Trimmed "the Van resistance" from another cluster to stay within the node ceiling this required. Added a line to the learning introduction naming the resulting hub structure explicitly.

### Round 14: Naming the impulse instead of gesturing at it

> "It's dehumanization itself that is the dark impulse, I want to say, and we should name that? [...] It's almost as if we could call it a single impulse under different given justifications for 'less than human'?"

**Resolution:** Agreed the previous framing ("probably not one impulse at all") dodged a pattern already sitting in the puzzle's own board — four unconnected eras independently reaching for the identical underlying move. Rewrote the introduction's opening around "one impulse, many vocabularies," naming dehumanization directly as the answer rather than hedging around it.

### Round 15: The delivery undercut the claim

> "It's dehumanization itself that is the dark impulse..." [caricatured as] "Hey, there may be—I mean there just might be—exactly one common impulse, and I'm getting ready to name it for you? Are you ready? I'm cutting in line in front of all the genocide scholars..."

**Resolution:** Recognized a standing, previously-banked principle being violated in real time: theatrical build-up before stating a settled finding, instead of stating it plainly and attributing it to the field. Rewrote the opening again — same claim, same confidence, no drumroll: "Genocide studies has settled on an answer, and this puzzle uses its name."

### Round 16: A precise terminology correction

> "This is a significant improvement. When you use the term 'collective guilt' what you really mean is 'collective blame'?"

**Resolution:** Agreed the distinction mattered beyond synonymy: guilt is an internal or self-recognized state; blame is an external judgment imposed regardless of actual responsibility. Using "guilt" risked quietly adopting the perpetrators' own framing that the group really was guilty. Renamed the bridge's id, term, fact, and lens references throughout.

## Part 4: Lens design discipline

### Round 17: Trivia instead of insight

> "Another issue I wanted to raise is the lenses. When you focus on collecting events you make this more like a cheap trivia puzzle than a vehicle for real insight?"

**Resolution:** Two of three lenses asked players to sort terms by which historical case they came from — pure provenance recall, answerable without understanding any mechanism. Replaced both with lenses requiring actual reasoning: *named-decisions* (an attributable, dated decision versus a diffuse belief or action) and a forerunner of what became the dehumanization-reach lens.

### Round 18: Schema vocabulary leaking into player text

> "'...or are a mechanism built specifically to extend that declaration into another part of the board?' WTF?"

**Resolution:** "The board" is authoring vocabulary — the player never sees the puzzle described in its own schema terms. Rewrote the prompt to name actual conceptual content (administrative record, blame attribution) instead of the puzzle's internal graph structure, and proactively found and fixed a second instance of the identical leak ("wired to more than one cluster... the board's connective layer") sitting in a different lens's explanation text.

### Round 19: The fix was still an enumeration

> [Quoting the still-compound lens text] "This isn't working out?"

**Resolution:** Diagnosed the prompt as testing three separate conditions stitched together with "either/or" rather than one criterion — doing the explanation's job inside the question itself. Collapsed it to a single test ("only function because that declaration has already been made") and flagged, unprompted, that the underlying lens might have been reverse-engineered from a target set rather than built from a real question, offering to cut it outright if the simplified version still didn't hold up.

### Round 20: A blanket rule, then a stricter one

> "The 'and' and 'or' connectors between clauses are suspicious of padding going on. [...] I'm going to declare 'and' and 'or' off limits until further notice!"
>
> "No! No comma lists! Lenses get a single clause. Short, non-compound, simple."

**Resolution:** Catalogued every remaining "and"/"or" across all three lenses' prompts as instances of the same padding pattern, then tightened further to eliminate comma-separated enumerations as well, not just conjunctions — addressed together in the redesign that followed.

### Round 21: Verify, don't assume

> "You listen to me. I don't know what you're up to, but there's something gone wrong. I've re-deployed authoring guidance in case it was out of date; please check it again. There's no longer a 3-target minimum; I don't know how that was ever approved for the guidance. The new minimum is 1."

**Resolution:** Called `get_authoring_guidance` directly rather than accepting or resisting the claim on faith. Confirmed the live guidance did state a 1–6 range and, independently, an explicit instruction against concatenating a second clause to recruit another cluster's terms — the exact pattern driving every round in this section. Split the still-compound lens into two honest single-clause lenses (*the-declaration*, 4 targets; *built-on-the-declaration*, 2 targets) and rewrote all four final prompts to zero "and"/"or" connectors and no comma lists.

## Part 5: Schema mechanics, prose precision, and sourcing

### Round 22: A symptom without yet a diagnosis

> "It's unusual that none of the lenses in the puzzle have displayed facts upon submission?"

**Resolution:** Investigated with the tools available rather than guessing at rendering behavior from outside it. Surfaced a real process issue along the way: the authoring guidance, freshly reloaded, explicitly said not to auto-submit after every edit without being asked — a rule the session had been violating for several rounds. Distinguished the drafts page (design-copy review) from the PR's branch preview (gameplay review) as the likely source of the confusion, and asked which one John meant rather than continuing to speculate.

### Round 23: The missing field, not missing content

> "Sorry, I didn't mean explanation, either; I meant 'reasons'. I'm looking at the PR itself in its current form. I'm also looking at the current draft. Reasons are missing in both places."

**Resolution:** Checked the schema's own canonical example and found a structural misuse running through every lens on the puzzle: per-target justification had been crammed into `explanation` as a list of sentences, while `reasons` — the field actually meant to carry that content — sat empty on all four lenses. Restructured every lens to split a short, general `explanation` from specific, per-target `reasons`. A validation error caught a mismatched key (`"banality of evil"` versus the actual target string `"the banality of evil"`) on the first attempt, fixed on the second.

### Round 24: An awkward sentence, precisely diagnosed

> "'...Both presuppose the claim already exists rather than making it themselves.' This reads awkwardly?"

**Resolution:** Identified the specific grammatical fault — mixing a static relation ("presuppose") with an active contrast ("rather than making") that implied a choice being made rather than a fact being stated. Replaced with two short, independent clauses: "Neither makes the claim -- both depend on it already having been made."

### Round 25: A word that overclaimed

> "'...what common ground, if any, explains all of them...' Explains isn't the right word here."

**Resolution:** "Explains" asserts causal-explanatory sufficiency — a stronger claim than the puzzle actually makes, and one already walked back once by the Stanton/Browning corrections. Replaced with "connects," which also picked up an unplanned echo with "connectivity, not sequence" later in the same piece.

### Round 26: A word with the wrong connotation

> "I'm going to push back against the use of the term 'common ground' here as well, as that term has a positive connotation in most contexts?"

**Resolution:** Agreed — "common ground" is almost always used in a conciliatory register (finding common ground between opposing parties), and importing that warmth into a sentence about what five genocides share was a real mismatch, not a stylistic quibble. Replaced with "common thread."

### Round 27: The frame itself, not the wording

> "It's too folksy: '...raise an old question that grandpa used to bring up around the fire, while grandma sewed...'"

**Resolution:** Recognized the caricature was aimed at the whole "pose an old question, then dramatically supply the answer" structure, not at any single word choice — the same riddle-and-reveal shape that survived the earlier fix to its literal wording (Round 15). Dropped the frame entirely for a flat declarative opening: "Five genocides ... share no era, ideology, or geography. Genocide studies has a name for what they do share..."

### Round 28: Proportion, not just accuracy

> "You devote a relatively large amount of space and argument in the lesson to Christopher Browning... I wonder if taking such pains conveys the wrong emphasis overall?"

**Resolution:** Recognized that four separate rounds of precision-corrections (necessity versus centrality, level of analysis, sequencing, connectivity) had all landed in the same paragraph, which had grown to outweigh the paragraph stating the puzzle's actual positive claim. Compressed from eight sentences to four, trusting the existing citation to carry supporting detail a reader could pursue independently.

### Round 29: When compression removes the argument, not just the words

> "No, that compression makes it hard to follow the argument."

**Resolution:** Diagnosed the real fault: the cut had removed connective logic, not just length, leaving two claims (the architecture claim, the Browning finding) asserted side by side with nothing showing why one didn't contradict the other. Restored the missing link — Browning's subjects were already standing inside architecture that predated them — at the same compressed length as before.

### Round 30: Reconciling two correct-sounding claims

> "'Dehumanization isn't reliably first...' is an odd way to start a sentence that should emphasize that dehumanization is 'almost always' first, and dehumanization filters in when an individual is surrounded by it, even if every individual act can't be directly traced to it?"

**Resolution:** Distinguished two different claims that had been getting collapsed into one: the specific, dated, coined slogan (Untermenschen, inyenzi) genuinely isn't always first in the documented paper trail, per Stanton — but a more diffuse, ambient cultural prejudice almost certainly precedes the formal declaration in every case, since a slogan usually crystallizes a prejudice already circulating rather than inventing it. Restructured the paragraph to lead with the affirmative, broader claim and treat the documented-timing caveat as the narrower technical point it actually is.

### Round 31: The board is not the lesson

> "'What's consistent is connectivity, not sequence...' This is stepping outside the lesson to focus on the board. That makes me a bit uncomfortable?"

**Resolution:** Recognized this as the same schema-vocabulary leak caught and fixed once already (Round 18) — "cluster," "wired to," "board" — creeping back in over several rounds of edits to the same paragraph, compounded here by a more serious problem: the sentence previewed the puzzle's own bridge topology, something a learning introduction is specifically not supposed to do. Cut the sentence entirely rather than rephrase it, and swept the rest of the paragraph for the same leaked vocabulary.

### Round 32: An unexamined psychological term

> "The other thing that makes me uncomfortable is introducing the term 'hate' here without defining what it means? Maybe that's another puzzle?"

**Resolution:** Recognized "hatred," "disgust," and "contempt" were being used as a settled emotional taxonomy nowhere actually defined, sourced, or grounded on the board — the same failure mode as an unexamined euphemism, just dressed as psychology instead of jargon. Cut the claim, keeping only what the banality-of-evil bridge (already cited to Arendt) already supported on its own. Agreed the taxonomy itself could be a strong, separately-sourced future puzzle rather than a three-word aside here.

### Round 33: Verifying a claim about a source's own standing

> "We might source Arendt before we source Samantha Power? We had this discussion in another thread. Using Samantha Power as a primary source is fraught within the peace studies community?"

**Resolution:** Searched prior conversations rather than assuming context, then verified the substantive claim directly rather than accepting it on faith — found a Peace and Conflict Studies journal explicitly framing Power's genocide-intervention argument as sitting on "the fault lines of the field," and a separate critique naming her interventionist premise directly. Checked how the puzzle actually used each citation and found Power wasn't argued from anywhere in the text, unlike the other four. Removed her from `info.citations` entirely, kept her in `sources` as further reading, and reordered the remaining four citations alphabetically.

### Round 34: A UX concern solvable at the content layer

> "The problem with a lengthy further reading list is that it pushes the board down and reserves that space for the entire duration of gameplay."

**Resolution:** Found a fix available without touching any rendering code: `citations` exists as a field on `learningIntroduction`, not just on the puzzle itself, and renders inside the dismissible Lesson dialog rather than the persistent page. Moved the full citation list there. Flagged honestly that the harder half of the request — what to do for puzzles with no lesson to move citations into — isn't something answerable from inside the authoring tools, and needs Claude Code to confirm actual rendering behavior before becoming a standing rule.

## How these puzzles changed

**Naming rather than softening.** The first four rounds share one shape: dramatic pacing, an unexamined euphemism, and missing historical depth are all forms of the same failure — describing something without actually naming what it is. The fix in each case was the same move: state the mechanism, name the euphemism, supply the years that were missing, rather than let atmosphere or omission do quieter work than a direct sentence would.

**A puzzle series is an argument, not a shelf.** Rounds 5 through 9 turned a density complaint into a completely different artifact. The honest answer to "are we doing one per genocide" was no — and the right response to a missing mechanism wasn't a bigger single-event puzzle, it was recognizing the mechanism belonged in a different kind of puzzle entirely, one built to compare rather than to document one case exhaustively.

**"Central" and "necessary" are different claims, and only one of them was actually contested.** Round 11 could have gone two ways: cave to the challenge, or double down on an unqualified claim. Neither was right. The precise fix was distinguishing which specific claim had real scholarly tension (sequencing/necessity) from which didn't (centrality) — and Round 12 immediately showed that even the "conceded" point had been mis-scoped, since the evidence cited was answering a different question (individual psychology) than the one the puzzle was actually making a claim about (systemic architecture).

**The board has to say what the prose says.** Round 13 is the session's sharpest catch: an argument about hierarchy that only exists in the learning introduction, while the actual game mechanic treats every cluster identically, isn't really an argument the puzzle makes — it's an argument the puzzle merely narrates. The fix had to happen in bridge connectivity, not in another sentence.

**Correct claims can still be delivered badly.** Rounds 14 and 15 are a matched pair: first, a claim that needed to be stated more directly; immediately after, a demonstration that the more direct version had swung into performing its own directness. The same underlying finding survived both corrections unchanged — only the packaging around it kept needing to be stripped back further.

**Lens padding was the dominant, recurring failure of this entire session.** Rounds 17 through 21 are five separate corrections to what turned out to be one habit: reaching for "and"/"or" and comma-separated lists to stretch a target set toward a size that felt more finished, rather than asking one honest question and accepting however many terms actually answered it. `validate_puzzle_draft`'s equal-count and whole-cluster flags fired more than once during this stretch, and each time the count was checked and rationalized as legitimate — which it usually was — without noticing that the padding mechanism itself (the compound clause producing the count) was the thing that needed inspection, not just the resulting number. The flags were catching a symptom; the actual rule wasn't checked directly against the live source until Round 21 asked for it.

**Verify claims about tool and guidance behavior directly.** Round 21's resolution — call the tool rather than reason about what it probably still says — is the same lesson this project has already learned once before, about the Library search box. A design constraint that's been treated as fixed for an entire session is exactly the kind of assumption worth re-checking against the live source the moment its currency is in doubt, rather than either accepting or resisting a claim about it secondhand.

**The schema sometimes already has the fix — the gap was in how the fields were used, not what content was missing.** Rounds 22–23 turned what looked like a missing-content problem ("no facts displayed") into a discovery that `reasons`, a field that had existed the whole time, had simply never been used — a whole category of "something feels off" resolved by using the tool correctly rather than adding anything new.

**Fixing one problem in a sentence doesn't guarantee the replacement is clean.** Rounds 25 through 27 corrected the same two sentences three times in a row — a word that overclaimed, then a word with the wrong connotation, then the surviving sentence structure itself. Each fix was real and necessary; none of them, on its own, was sufficient.

**Compression can delete an argument while only intending to delete its length.** Rounds 28 and 29 are a clean before-and-after: cutting supporting material to fix a proportion problem silently removed the connective logic that made the paragraph an argument rather than a list of assertions. The fix wasn't reverting the cut — it was restoring the logic at the same short length.

**Two well-earned corrections can turn out to be in tension, and resolving that takes a genuinely new distinction, not a choice between them.** Round 30's "ambient versus documented dehumanization" distinction didn't exist in any earlier round — it had to be built fresh to reconcile the puzzle's own hub-connectivity design (Round 13) with the Stanton-sourced finding that classification can precede a coined slogan.

**A schema-vocabulary leak isn't a one-time fix — it recurs in new prose even after being named and corrected once.** Round 31 caught the identical "cluster/wired/board" pattern from Round 18 reappearing across several rounds of edits to the same paragraph, this time compounded by a second problem — previewing board structure — the first occurrence hadn't raised.

**An unsupported technical term is an unsupported claim, whether it's jargon or psychology.** Round 32 applied the exact discipline established for "close air support" in Part 1 to a completely different kind of unexamined vocabulary — an emotional taxonomy (hatred, disgust, contempt) with real academic literature behind it that the puzzle never cited.

**Source verification extends to a source's own reception, not just its content.** Round 33 is a new dimension on an established principle: it's not enough to verify what a citation says — its positioning as a primary or lead source can itself be a contested editorial choice, and that contest is worth checking against real scholarship rather than taking on faith in either direction.

**Not every UX complaint needs code — but knowing which ones do requires honesty about the boundary.** Round 34 solved a real rendering concern entirely through content architecture (a different schema field), while explicitly declining to guess at the harder case that actually does require checking the application's source.

## Where *The Grammar of Genocide* stands now

Thirty-four rounds in, the puzzle bears little resemblance to what PR #148 first contained.

- **Structure.** What began as four parallel clusters became five, with dehumanization restructured as the only cluster wired to all four others — an argument about hierarchy the board's own bridge topology now makes, not just the prose sitting beside it.
- **Claims.** Every load-bearing sentence in the learning introduction has been checked against a real source at least once and revised at least once — Bandura's actual mechanism split, Stanton's actual stage order, Browning's actual argument, and two sequencing claims (classification, collective blame) corrected to reflect that the puzzle's own cases run in both directions, not one.
- **Lenses.** From three lenses built on case-provenance recall — trivia — to four lenses that each require one honest, single-clause piece of reasoning, with `reasons` and `explanation` finally doing the separate jobs they were built for instead of one field trying to do both badly.
- **Prose discipline.** The introduction no longer performs discovery, doesn't borrow warmth from the wrong register, states its central claim in plain declarative sentences, and never asks a player to reason about the puzzle's own internal machinery instead of the history it's actually teaching.
- **Sourcing.** Five citations became four, ordered by actual argumentative use rather than the sequence they were first added in, with the one citation whose own scholarly positioning was contested moved to further reading rather than dropped without examination or left unquestioned.

The throughline across nearly every round is the same, and worth stating plainly: almost none of this was caught by `validate_puzzle_draft`, which is exactly as it should be. The validator catches structural violations — not whether a claim is true, whether a word carries the right connotation, or whether an argument survives being shortened. That judgment stayed entirely human, round after round, and the puzzle is substantially stronger for having gone through it.
