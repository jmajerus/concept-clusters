# Pushback & Resolutions: *The Accretive Health Scandal* Drafting Session

A record of every point in this thread where John pushed back on a Claude proposal, and how each was resolved.

This session runs in six movements: whether the subject deserved a puzzle at all, and where it belonged if so; building the coercion mechanism and finding vocabulary equal to it; a long, repeated refusal to let dollar figures smuggle false equivalence between patient harm and investor harm onto the board; recasting the entire puzzle around bioethics once that was named as its real category; a term-length pass to fit the board; and a final, nine-revision struggle over a single bridge fact — what happened to the practice after the ban that stopped it — that took longer to get right than any other sentence in the puzzle.

---

## Part 1: Whether this was the right project, and for what

### Round 1: A format objection

Before a single node existed, John questioned the premise.

> "Looking at the facts here, I don't dispute any of them, but the puzzle format here appears soulless, and I'd surmise this story is better told in article format, perhaps with the aide of Claude web?"

**Resolution:** No puzzle edit yet — this set the terms for what followed rather than getting argued away. The concern stood on its own merits; a sorting puzzle doesn't automatically carry the moral weight a written account can. Rather than defend the format on principle, the conversation moved to the actual question underneath it: what a puzzle contributes that prose doesn't (cross-referencing a mechanism against the laws it breaks, the accountability that followed, and the harm that outlasted it, in one object a player has to actively sort rather than passively read), and where that object should actually live.

### Round 2: Where this puzzle actually belongs

> "There's a lot to cover in the Anatomy of Coercion and Conscience meta-catalogue, including the puzzles you mentioned. There isn't a similar catalogue for economic exploitation and its solutions. We could draft this puzzle to hold in reserve for when that catalogue is designed?"

**Resolution:** Agreed, and treated it as the actual answer to Round 1 rather than a separate decision: this puzzle wasn't competing with an article for the same audience — it was infrastructure for a meta-catalogue that doesn't exist yet. The puzzle proceeded on that basis: built and sourced, but understood from the start as parked rather than queued for immediate publication.

### Round 3: Extraction feels like abuse

> "You use the word 'extraction', but, to the patient in their most vulnerable state (sick and in a hospital gown) it feels like abuse. The result of that experience is fear of returning for necessary treatment, resulting in actual harm — harm up to and including death?"

**Resolution:** This single message shaped an entire cluster before the board existed. "Extraction" is an economist's word for what happened to money; it says nothing about what happened to the patient's relationship to care afterward. That distinction became *The Harm That Outlasts the Bill* — care avoidance, financial toxicity, delayed emergency care, sanctuary trauma, and institutional betrayal — built specifically to name the harm in the patient's own terms rather than the vendor's.

## Part 2: Building the mechanism and its vocabulary

### Round 4: An unexamined design-failure framing

> "We closely examined and eliminated euphemisms that laundered military terms that concealed killing and other harms behind neutral language. Are we using industry language here without question? The opening fact on Mechanisms of Extraction cluster reads 'Accretive Health didn't just fail to stop aggressive collectors...' is a bit vague in that to an uninitiated reader it may seem as if 'stopping aggressive collectors' was their original business model rather than a reluctantly received court order?"

**Resolution:** Applied a standing project principle — don't accept industry-supplied framing at face value — to a case where the failure was subtler than a single euphemistic word: the sentence structure itself implied Accretive had merely failed to rein in a problem, when the sourced record (Swanson's court filing) showed the coercion was the designed operating model from day one. Rewrote the fact to state that plainly: "pressuring patients for payment mid-treatment was the designed operating model from the start" — a framing that survived, largely unchanged, to the final board.

### Round 5: Documentation and compulsion aren't rival teams

> "It's not documented vs. compelled: those two things are on the same team?"

**Resolution:** Agreed the cluster's fact text had drifted into treating the AG report and Senate hearing as if they competed with the settlement for credit, when they're actually different stages of one accountability effort — the report and hearing built the record; the settlement is what the record made possible. Rewrote the *How Far the Reckoning Reached* fact to state that relationship as sequence and difference in kind (only one had power to compel anything), not as opposing camps.

### Round 6: Naming the tactic in its own words

> "We're adding a new term: 'Accretive Secret Sauce'?"

**Resolution:** Verified the term against Swanson's own court filing before adding it — the company's internal materials really did use that name and the "you've never seen ASS like ours" tagline. Added it as a seed term alongside "bedside payment demand," letting the company's own branding of its tactic sit on the board rather than only a clinical description of it.

### Round 7: Vocabulary for both sides of the same interaction

> "I want you to dig a bit deeper yet. Are there psychological terms for what the act of accosting people who are at their most vulnerable, and in a forceful manner, that can be ascribed to either party in the interaction?"

**Resolution:** Researched Kipnis's and Luna's bioethics vulnerability taxonomy and found the Belmont Report itself already names both sides in one definition. Added **situational vulnerability** (the patient's side: a temporary, context-produced state) and **undue influence** (the collector's side: exploiting a position of authority over someone in that state) — the pair that later became this puzzle's first matched lens, and the detail that eventually justified reclassifying the whole puzzle as bioethics (Round 19).

### Round 8: The mirror pair for lasting harm

> "If there were more room, I'd consider adding the notion of 'feeling unsafe': a patient goes to place where they expect to receive compassionate care, and what they experience initially is the opposite of that?"

**Resolution:** Added **sanctuary trauma** (a place expected to be protective becoming the source of harm instead) and **institutional betrayal** (Freyd's term for harm done by an institution someone depends on) to *The Harm That Outlasts the Bill*, deliberately mirroring the situational-vulnerability/undue-influence pair from Round 7 — one names the patient's felt experience, one names the institutional mechanism, in both clusters. This became the puzzle's second matched lens.

## Part 3: Refusing to let the numbers say what they don't

### Round 9: An unverifiable statistic

> "'...boasted a 15 percent collection yield...' Is that a 15 percent boost in collection yield? That would be different than how it reads now? Also, if how it reads now is correct, the 15 percent number is essentially meaningless without a baseline to compare it to?"

**Resolution:** Attempted five separate verifications (Becker's, HealthcareInfoSecurity, the 7th Circuit opinion, a tracking blog) — all failed or didn't mention the figure. Concluded the number couldn't be verified as either an absolute or a lift-over-baseline, and removed it entirely rather than guess, keeping only the independently corroborated tagline quote.

### Round 10: The scale check

> "And while we're looking at figures, the settlement amount with the State of Minnesota seems like a lot until you look at the CEO's salary shortly thereafter: [Healthcare Dive link, $20.6M first-year pay]"

**Resolution:** Verified the figure and added it as its own term, **CEO's $20.6M pay**, with a fact tying it directly to the settlement: Accretive's incoming CEO was paid nearly nine times the entire settlement in a single year. This is the term that later prompted the AI-splaining corrections in Rounds 13 and 16, once the temptation arose to explain to the reader why that comparison mattered instead of just stating it.

### Round 11: What could be cut

> "What are one or two nodes that might be cut without significant impact if we needed to?"

**Resolution:** Proposed candidates, including merging the two settlements (state and shareholder) into fewer nodes — the proposal that immediately ran into Rounds 12–15.

### Round 12: Suppressing the pill, not the fact

> "I disagree. I think that the 2 settlement nodes could be folded together and the dollar amounts thus supressed on the board, because they are misleading due to what we just talked about?" — followed by: "Before you come back at me, I meant suppressed on the playing surface as part of the node term labels (which need to be short anyway on a board of this size) and placed in the info text, not concealed from the reader?"

**Resolution:** Agreed and drew the distinction correctly: the dollar figures stay in the sourced `termInfo` text, in full, but leave the short board-facing term label — the labels aren't the place for numbers that read as more meaningful out of context than they are.

### Round 13: AI-splaining, first offense

> "What exactly are you doing with the following sentence, and why did you feel the need to AI-splain (Hey, I may have coined a new term?) [...] 'The size of each number reflects what was being litigated, not which harm mattered more.'"

**Resolution:** Recognized the sentence for what it was — narrating the reasoning behind a comparison instead of just stating the comparison and trusting the reader — and cut it. This exchange is the one that coined the term now tracked as a standing project pattern (see [[feedback_reduce_editorial_hedging]]).

### Round 14: "Dual-track" implies coordination

> "Also, the term 'dual-track' implies some sort of coordination, when clearly there was none?"

**Resolution:** Agreed — the state settlement (patient harm) and the shareholder settlement (investor harm) were unrelated litigation on unrelated injuries; "dual-track" accidentally suggested they were two coordinated prongs of one strategy. Dropped the term.

### Round 15: Equal weight, still

> "I'm even uncomfortable with the term 'separate settlements', because it unintentionally gives equal weight to investor concerns as patient concerns." — then, after a proposed two-node compromise: "No! Absolutely not. Two side-by-side nodes are doing the same thing as 'separate settlements' was doing. This is about patient harm. You're not smuggling investor harm into this as something of equal stature?"

**Resolution:** Stopped trying to find a neutral-sounding label and removed the shareholder settlement as a board term entirely. It now exists only as a subordinate clause inside the **state settlement** term's own `termInfo`: "A separate, unrelated shareholder lawsuit over securities-fraud claims also settled in 2013, for $14 million — a different harm entirely, borne by investors rather than patients." This is a standing constraint on the puzzle going forward, not a one-time fix.

### Round 16: AI-splaining, second offense

> "Here you go, AI-splainin' again: 'The figure isn't evidence of wrongdoing; it's a scale check on the state settlement.'"

**Resolution:** Same failure as Round 13, on the same term's neighbor sentence. Cut it. Two rounds on the same pattern in the same cluster is its own evidence the habit needed a name, not just a fix.

### Round 17: The word "just"

> "'Which of these accountability events actually forced Accretive to do something, rather than just building a public record?' I take issue with the use of the word 'just' here?"

**Resolution:** Agreed — "just building a public record" trivializes work (Swanson's investigation, the Senate hearing) the puzzle's own thesis treats as real, substantive accountability, even without compulsory power behind it. Reworded to remove the diminishment.

### Round 18: The worst lens question

> "I've never seen such a poor example of lens question than this one, for multiple reasons."

**Resolution:** The `documented-vs-compelled` lens — carried over from the original inventory's rival-distinction merge (Round 5) — turned out to be structurally broken: a two-part question with a one-part answer, missing the cluster's fourth term, and answering itself in language already stated verbatim in the cluster's own fact. Deleted the lens rather than re-patch wording that couldn't fix a structural problem.

## Part 4: Recasting the whole board as bioethics

### Round 19: Moving the primary category

> "Because I've decided on a primary place for this puzzle that's different than yours: Medicine | Bioethics? I wouldn't normally make it the primary home, but business and organizations is already getting bloated by contrast?"

**Resolution:** Changed the puzzle's primary category to Physiology & Medicine, kept Business & Organizations as a secondary category. Since "Bioethics" wasn't a registered subcategory under Physiology & Medicine, added it to `category-backlog.json` instead of setting an invalid `subcategories` value on the draft — a gap to close if or when a bioethics-first puzzle actually lands.

### Round 20: A full pass through the new lens

> "Because this is now declared to be a bioethics puzzle, a review pass over the entire board with that in mind is in order?"

**Resolution:** Reviewed every cluster fact and `termInfo` entry against the bioethics reframe. The two matched pairs already on the board from Rounds 7 and 8 — situational-vulnerability/undue-influence and sanctuary-trauma/institutional-betrayal — became the puzzle's two formal lenses during this pass, each with its own prompt, reasons, and explanation tying the pair back to the Belmont Report's own language.

### Round 21: Rebalancing fact against lesson

> "Some of the cluster facts are rather lengthy and could be partially refactored into the lesson text? As in some of the detail can go in the lesson text while a summary stays in the facts? And I'm especially concerned in regard that the most salient points related to the bioethics concerns aren't buried in one or two of a collection of equally weighted cluster nodes?"

**Resolution:** Moved the Belmont Report's own definitions of situational vulnerability and undue influence, and the framing of EMTALA as the legal answer to that vulnerability, up into the learning introduction's own paragraph — stated once, prominently, before any cluster — rather than leaving the bioethics argument implicit in one cluster's fact among four peers.

### Round 22: The opening sentence's own equal-weighting problem

> "I want to call out the very opening sentence of the lesson text: 'Between 2010 and 2012, a Chicago-based vendor called Accretive Health embedded its own staff inside two Minnesota hospital systems to collect payment from patients — sometimes at the bedside, sometimes mid-treatment.' Remember our discussions about giving things equal weight that don't necessarily deserve equal weight?"

**Resolution:** Caught the same false-parity failure from Part 3, now at the level of sentence structure rather than board nodes: "sometimes at the bedside, sometimes mid-treatment" reads as two comparably ordinary contexts, when the point is that patients were pressured at the exact moment they were least able to refuse. Rewrote the opening to state that directly: "to pressure patients for payment at the moment they were least able to refuse — mid-crisis, in pain, alone."

## Part 5: Fitting the board

### Round 23: Flagging the long terms

> "There are many long terms on this board that are likely to make fitting difficult. Before I attempt to do so in a PR, are there any you might see that can be trimmed in advance without negative impact?"

**Resolution:** Proposed a full list of trims across both clusters and bridges — "embedded collector staffing" → "embedded collectors," "HIPAA business-associate liability" → "HIPAA business associate," "Minnesota debt-collection law" → "Minnesota debt-collection," "Senate HELP subcommittee hearing" → "Senate HELP hearing," "Swanson six-volume AG report" → "Swanson's AG report," "CEO's $20.6M first-year pay" → "CEO's $20.6M pay," "care-avoidance behavior" → "care avoidance," "delayed emergency care risk" → "delayed emergency care," plus bridge terms "business-associate liability" → "business-associate status," "landmark enforcement precedent" → "enforcement precedent," "operating ban as remedy" → "operating ban," and "the hearing's stated fear" → "the hearing's fear."

### Round 24: Applying the trims, with two exceptions

> "Yes, please apply the entire set of trims listed, except keep '\'deadbeats\' internal culture' and 'coercive billing encounter'?"

**Resolution:** Applied every trim from Round 23 except the two named exceptions, which stayed at full length.

### Round 25: One of the exceptions was still wrong

> "I'm uncomfortable with the term 'billing encounter' as it's just kinda weird? What exactly is a billing encounter [...] Now, if the term was 'collections encounter' that's something that people might more easily visualize? But where the patient is forced to 'pay in advance' that truly is a billing encounter, as 'collections' suggests a past-due balance?"

**Resolution:** The bridge term explicitly kept unchanged one round earlier turned out to have a real problem unrelated to length: "billing encounter" doesn't clearly evoke anything, and "collections encounter" would have been actively wrong (it implies a past-due balance, not pay-in-advance coercion). Renamed to **coercive payment encounter**, which names the actual dynamic — payment demanded before or during treatment — precisely.

## Part 6: The fact that got rewritten nine times

One bridge — `bridge-two-year-ban`, connecting the embedded-collector tactic to the state settlement that stopped it — went through more revisions than any other single field in the puzzle. It is worth tracing start to finish.

### Round 26: An awkward appositive

> "'fact: The Minnesota settlement didn't just fine Accretive — it specifically barred the embedded-collector operating model from the state for two years, aiming at the mechanism itself rather than only its output.' The appositive is awkwardly worded?"

**Resolution:** Rewrote the trailing appositive as a "not just X" clause instead: "...it barred the embedded-collector model itself from the state for two years, not just the money it had extracted." This fix created a new, worse problem, caught immediately in the next round.

### Round 27: The fix that banned money

> "Banning the money it had extracted? That's a very strange way of saying banning Accretive itself?"

**Resolution:** The new sentence's structure made "barred" read as applying to both the model *and* the money — as if the settlement had banned currency. Dropped the trailing clause entirely, since "didn't just fine Accretive" already carried the fine-versus-ban contrast on its own: "The Minnesota settlement didn't just fine Accretive — it barred the embedded-collector model itself from the state for two years."

### Round 28: A closed case that wasn't

> "It's been significantly longer than two years now; did these practices return to the state after that two years had lapsed? [...] This happened then. What's changed since?"

**Resolution:** Researched the settlement's actual terms directly (FTC.gov, two independent settlement summaries): the two-year ban was a floor, not the whole restriction — a four-year AG-approval condition ran through November 2018. Accretive itself didn't survive that window under its old name: CEO ousted in 2013, a 20-year FTC data-security consent order finalized in 2014, a full rebrand to R1 RCM in 2017. Rewrote the fact to include this — but stopped at "the company dissolved" as the answer, which the next round correctly identified as not actually answering the question.

### Round 29: What "old shenanigans" actually requires

> "Wait, that's scarcely what people want to know? Is the rebranded company up to its old shenanigans again or not?"

**Resolution:** Researched R1 RCM's current state directly rather than treating the rebrand as a natural ending: R1 now serves over 1,000 hospitals and health systems nationwide, was taken private in 2024 for $8.9 billion, and lists patient collection at the point of care among its current services. No evidence the specific embedded-collector tactic recurred under that name — but the CFPB was still issuing industry-wide advisory warnings about revenue-cycle firms' collection abuses as recently as October 2024. Rewrote the fact to state that continuity plainly: identity died, the underlying business model didn't.

### Round 30: The lesson promised what the board didn't deliver

> "The lesson text suggests a resolution that the board doesn't deliver. Buried in the 'operating ban' bridge term is this: 'the CFPB was still issuing advisory warnings about revenue-cycle firms' collection abuses as recently as October 2024. The tactic didn't survive; the underlying business did.'"

**Resolution:** The learning introduction still read as a closed historical arc ("became a legal and political reckoning that ran through... a settlement") while the actual unresolved ending sat only in one optional connector most players might never open. Added a fourth paragraph to the introduction stating the non-resolution directly — "None of this ends where it looks like it should" — and updated the introduction's own `summary` field to match, so the framing itself sets the right expectation before a player ever reaches a bridge.

### Round 31: A smaller wording fix, in passing

> "'info: The senator running the hearing wasn't reacting to a hypothetical — he named the exact outcome the evidence in this cluster then went on to document.' [...] I want to keep this point, but refine the wording a bit, perhaps?"

**Resolution:** A different bridge (`bridge-frankens-fear`), caught in passing during the same stretch of close review. "The evidence in this cluster then went on to document" was a clunky double-verb chain. Tightened to "he named exactly the outcome this cluster's evidence documents," keeping the same claim and the puzzle's established "this cluster" idiom for bridge `info` text.

### Round 32: A pronoun with no antecedent yet

> "'The ban stopped that specific tactic in Minnesota...' It's not clear what 'that specific tactic' means here when we say a few sentences further on '...with patient collection at the point of care listed among its current services...' [...] Perhaps we should move 'embedded-collector' up in the text and not have the pronoun 'that specific tactic' come first?"

**Resolution:** Reordered the fact so "embedded-collector tactic" is named in the opening sentence, before "point of care" is introduced as the separate, broader, still-current practice — so the later backward reference to "that specific tactic" has an unambiguous antecedent instead of inviting confusion with the different thing named in between.

### Round 33: Still too far away

> "Actually, the pronoun is too far away from the first sentence. Try again."

**Resolution:** Stopped relying on a pronoun across the intervening sentence entirely and restated the noun instead: "No evidence the embedded-collector tactic itself recurred under the new name." Distance stopped mattering once the reference no longer depended on distance.

### Round 34: Who actually does the collecting

> "So what has me scratching my head here is that if 'patient collection at the point of care' is a current service, but there is no longer an 'embedded collector', then who is doing the collection at the point of care, exactly?"

**Resolution:** This was the load-bearing question the vaguer "point of care" phrasing had been inviting all along. Researched R1's own careers page directly rather than guessing: R1 still employs "Patient Registration Representatives" on-site inside hospital emergency departments and outpatient registration desks today — the identical embedding structure Accretive built, rebranded from "collections" to "patient support" messaging. No confirmed evidence (R1's own marketing wouldn't say either way) that these staff demand payment pre-stabilization the way Accretive's did. Rewrote the fact and the learning introduction to name this precisely instead of the vaguer phrasing that had invited the question.

### Round 35: The revelation belonged in the lesson, not the connector

> "This revelation is something that should be primarily in the lesson text, not buried in the now even longer bridge fact?"

**Resolution:** The full R1/CFPB epilogue had ended up duplicated in both the learning introduction and an increasingly bloated bridge fact. Moved it to live only in the introduction — where a thesis-level point actually belongs, ahead of the board rather than inside one optional connector — and trimmed the bridge back down to what a bridge is for: the mechanism connecting "embedded collectors" to "state settlement" (fine versus structural ban, and the AG-approval condition through 2018).

### Round 36: Trimmed too far

> "I think you trimmed the bridge fact too severely. Could we compromise here? Or just restore the full prior fact if a partly trimmed version won't read well?"

**Resolution:** The trim had gone all the way to pure settlement mechanics, leaving the bridge with no hook into anything past 2018. Added back one closing clause — "The company that would have needed that permission didn't survive intact long enough to ask" — that plants the continuation without re-narrating the R1/CFPB specifics, which stayed exclusively in the introduction.

### Round 37: The wrong verb for what mattered

> "I don't think 'survival' is the right word here, when we're elevating the survival of the name above the survival of both the general physical aggregation and general ethos?"

**Resolution:** "Didn't survive intact" used the entity's identity as the thing whose continuity was being measured — exactly backward from the puzzle's own point, established a paragraph earlier, that the name is what died while the staffing structure and ethos persisted. Reworded to state a name change rather than a death: "The company that would have needed that permission had, by then, changed its name — not what it did." This closing line now echoes, rather than contradicts, the introduction's own "The company's name changed. The staffing model didn't."

## How this puzzle changed

Thirty-seven rounds across six movements is a lot for one board, and it's worth being honest about what that says rather than smoothing it over. `validate_puzzle_draft` caught none of this — nothing here was a schema violation. Every one of these was an editorial judgment call, and nearly every one of them made the puzzle say something truer than the version before it.

**False neutrality, caught at every scale.** The pattern from Round 4 (a passive "didn't just fail to stop" framing for a tactic that was the designed business model) recurred at the sentence level in Round 22 (a parallel "sometimes... sometimes..." construction flattening bedside coercion into an ordinary billing variant) and at the word level in Round 5 (treating documentation and compulsion as rivals rather than stages of one effort). Each time, the fix was the same: state what the sourcing actually supports, not the version that sounds more measured.

**Refusing false equivalence, held under real pressure.** Rounds 11 through 15 are the sharpest single thread in the session — five successive attempts (merge, suppress-the-number, rename to "separate," reduce to two side-by-side nodes) to give investor harm a place on the board that didn't quietly grant it patient harm's stature. The settlement finally landed as a subordinate clause inside a single term, and that placement is now a standing constraint on the puzzle, not a one-round fix.

**A pattern worth naming became a pattern worth watching for.** Rounds 13 and 16 are the same failure — narrating the reasoning behind a fact instead of stating the fact — caught twice in the same cluster before it stopped happening. Naming it "AI-splaining" made it recognizable enough to catch on sight afterward.

**One fact, revised nine times, because the story kept being more current than the last draft admitted.** Rounds 26 through 37 are a single connector's history: an awkward appositive led to a sentence that accidentally banned money, which led to a technically clean but historically closed sentence, which read as trivia because it stopped at 2014 instead of asking what happened after. Each subsequent round wasn't re-litigating the last one — it was surfacing a genuinely deeper layer of the same unresolved question (the settlement's real duration, then the company's own dissolution, then the industry's continuity, then who specifically still does the work, then where in the puzzle that fact should even live, then the exact word for what persisted). The puzzle now says something no earlier draft did: the specific illegal tactic has no confirmed recurrence under a new name, but the same staffing structure — Accretive's own employees, on-site, inside hospital emergency departments — never stopped, and regulators were still fighting the underlying pattern industry-wide as of October 2024. That is a materially more honest and more useful claim than "Minnesota banned it for two years," and it took ten rounds of pushback to get there because each round was correct about a real gap the previous one had left.

**The board and the lesson now agree with each other.** Round 30 caught a real structural failure mode that isn't specific to this puzzle: a learning introduction can promise closure while a single optional connector quietly contradicts it, and a player who never opens that connector never learns the puzzle doesn't think the story is over. Rounds 34 and 35 pushed that correction further — not just *that* something continues, but precisely *what* continues and *where in the puzzle* that claim should live, so the thesis is stated once, prominently, rather than duplicated or buried.

The puzzle that emerges from this is smaller in some places than any single draft along the way — no unverifiable statistic, no shareholder settlement sharing a term with the state's, a bridge fact trimmed back to exactly what a bridge is for — and larger in the one place that matters most: a learning introduction that tells a player, honestly, that this case didn't end in 2014, and a board that can back that claim up with a name, a job title, and a 2024 regulatory citation rather than a vague gesture at "the point of care."
